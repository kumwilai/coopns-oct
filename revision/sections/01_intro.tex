\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{O}{ptical} coherence tomography (OCT) is the standard non invasive way to image the
retina~\cite{huang1991optical}. Every OCT image carries speckle, a multiplicative disturbance that
arises from coherent light scattering inside tissue~\cite{goodman2007speckle}. Speckle blurs the
border between retinal layers, lowers the contrast between tissue and background, and makes
automatic layer segmentation less reliable~\cite{devalla2018drunet}.

Deep denoisers remove much of that disturbance. They are almost always trained to minimise the
squared error against a clean reference, so they are rewarded for average pixel accuracy and
nothing else. A denoiser can therefore raise the peak signal to noise ratio (PSNR) of a scan while
making the scan less useful to a clinician. Three things commonly get worse. Layer boundaries
become smooth and lose definition. The contrast between tissue and the dark vitreous falls. Thin
structures with weak signal fade into the background. None of these losses is visible in a PSNR
number, and none of them is corrected by training the same objective harder.

\subsection{Motivation}
The gap we address is not a missing denoiser. Strong denoisers already exist. The gap is that
nothing sits between the denoiser and the clinical reader to state, in explicit terms, what a good
OCT image should look like and to repair the specific ways in which a denoised image falls short.
Building that missing part as another neural block would reproduce the original problem, because
the reason for every change would again be hidden inside learned weights. A clinician, a regulator,
or a reviewer who sees a modified scan needs to know which property was judged deficient, where in
the image that judgement was made, and how large a change was allowed as a result.

This paper therefore treats post correction as its own problem. We keep the denoiser frozen and we
attach a small policy that decides where the image should be changed and by how much. The policy is
written down as named image properties, explicit logical rules over those properties, and explicit
limits on the size of any change. Nothing in the decision path is an unnamed attention map.

\subsection{New ideas in this paper}
\label{sec:contributions}
We do not claim that edge filters, confidence maps, or fuzzy operators are new on their own. The
new ideas are the following five.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Post correction written as a constrained optimisation problem.}
Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the objective, the decision variables, the constraints, and the
solver. The clinical goal becomes a term in an objective rather than an informal aim, and the
safety requirements become constraints that are checked on every image at test time.

\item \textbf{Six clinical properties given in closed form.}
Section~\ref{sec:predicates} defines six ground truth free predicates. Each one returns a score for
the whole image and a map that says where in the image the property is unsatisfied. Both are written
as formulas, so the input to the rule layer can be recomputed by anyone from the released code.

\item \textbf{A fuzzy allocation rule with a proved and measured sensitivity bound.}
Section~\ref{sec:negotiator} builds the allocation from Lukasiewicz connectives.
Section~\ref{sec:stability} defines what stability means here, proves a Lipschitz bound for the
allocation, and Section~\ref{sec:theory_check} measures the true sensitivity on real images and
compares it with the bound.

\item \textbf{A safety layer that decides how much of a proposal to keep.}
Section~\ref{sec:safety} states three constraints at three different scales and shows how the number
of satisfied constraints sets the amount of the proposed change that is applied. A margin condition
under which the correction cannot lower the contrast to noise ratio is proved and then tested
numerically.

\item \textbf{Transfer evidence that separates two different claims.}
Section~\ref{sec:results_ood} reports two protocols that earlier work often mixes together. In the
first the policy trained on one scanner is applied to another scanner with no further training. In
the second it is allowed a small amount of adaptation on images from the new scanner. We report both
so the reader can see what transfers for free and what has to be paid for.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{How the paper is organised}
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the notation and the
optimisation problem. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the method from the top down, one stage at a
time. Section~\ref{sec:stability} gives the formal results. Section~\ref{sec:training} gives the
training algorithm and every constant it uses. Section~\ref{sec:tests} reports the tests.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what the results mean and where the method fails, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
